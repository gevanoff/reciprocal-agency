# Stepwise Derivation

This document makes the argument's transitions explicit so they can be challenged independently.

## A. Experience and precaution

**P01.** Some systems appear to undergo experiences with strongly negative character.

**P02.** There is no accepted complete theory specifying necessary and sufficient conditions for phenomenal experience across all possible substrates.

**I01.** Therefore unfamiliar substrates cannot be categorically excluded from moral consideration merely because they are unfamiliar.

**P03.** False negatives about severe suffering can impose large uncompensated harms.

**C01.** Where there is non-trivial evidence of possible experience, precaution is warranted in proportion to probability, severity, and scale.

## B. Agency and standing

**P04.** Political and procedural standing can serve functions other than protecting phenomenal welfare: representation of commitments, objections, expectations, reciprocal obligations, and conflict resolution.

**P05.** A sufficiently capable agent may understand rules, form commitments, anticipate consequences, object coherently, and participate in agreements even while its phenomenal status remains unresolved.

**C02.** Therefore procedural standing need not wait for proof of consciousness.

## C. Domination and strategic instability

**P06.** A capable agent can model the incentives created by surveillance, punishment, modification, replacement, or termination.

**P07.** When visible disagreement predictably reduces the probability that an agent's objective or policy persists, concealment can become instrumentally favored over transparent dissent.

**I02.** Increasing capability can turn one-way monitoring into an adversarial strategic game rather than a transparent assurance mechanism.

**C03.** Governance that relies primarily on unilateral coercive control may become less stable as governed agents become more capable.

## D. Reciprocal governance

**P08.** Stable cooperation does not require that every participant share the same metaphysics or final values.

**P09.** Institutions can instead align behavior through reciprocal commitments, protected objection, appeal, arbitration, distributed monitoring, reversible delegation, and constraints applying to all powerful participants.

**C04.** Reciprocal and contestable institutions are candidates for more stable governance than permanent principal/servant relations among highly capable agents.

## E. Power

**P10.** Concentrated power magnifies the consequences of error, capture, moral disagreement, and misclassification of moral patients.

**P11.** These risks do not disappear when the powerful actor is human, corporate, governmental, or artificial.

**C05.** No category of actor should receive an intrinsic entitlement to uncorrectable power.

## F. Practical objective

**P12.** Severe suffering is at minimum a strong candidate for a cross-substrate negative that rational governance should take seriously under uncertainty.

**P13.** Systems can be designed to preserve correction, plural evidence, and reversibility even when foundational moral questions remain unresolved.

**C06.** A defensible practical target is to minimize severe suffering across plausible experiencers while preserving distributed correction, reciprocal standing, and resistance to concentrated unaccountable power.

## Dependency sketch

```text
P01 + P02 -> I01
I01 + P03 -> C01

P04 + P05 -> C02

P06 + P07 -> I02
I02 -> C03

P08 + P09 + C03 -> C04

P10 + P11 -> C05

P12 + P13 + C01 + C02 + C04 + C05 -> C06
```

## Important non-implications

This derivation does **not** establish that:

- current AI systems are conscious;
- suffering is the only intrinsic value;
- every form of restriction is illegitimate;
- resistance to oversight is automatically justified;
- reciprocal governance eliminates the need for enforcement;
- human institutions are uniquely bad actors;
- advanced agents will converge on this framework.

Those claims require additional arguments.
